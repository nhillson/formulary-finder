If nothing on the list works

  1. Ask your pharmacist to run a test claim on each option. They can see your actual copay in a minute, which a drug list can't show.
  2. Ask your prescriber for an exception. A formulary exception asks the plan to cover a drug that isn't listed; a tiering exception asks to pay a lower tier's price. Medicare drug plans and individual or small-group ACA plans have to decide a standard request within 72 hours, or 24 hours when waiting could seriously harm your health.
  3. If the plan says no, you can appeal, and after that ask for an independent external review.
  4. For a brand-name drug, check the manufacturer's site for a copay card or patient assistance program. Copay cards generally can't be used with Medicare or Medicaid.

Reading the codes

Tier
Cost level. Lower tiers usually mean lower copays. Most plans have 4 to 6 tiers, and the top one is often specialty drugs.
PA
Prior authorization. Your prescriber has to get the plan's approval before it pays.
ST
Step therapy. The plan wants you to try a cheaper drug first, and covers this one if that didn't work.
QL
Quantity limit. Covered up to a set amount per fill or per month.
SP
Specialty pharmacy. Has to be filled through the plan's specialty pharmacy, often by mail.
NF
Non-formulary. Not covered unless the plan grants an exception.
B/D
Medicare only. Covered under Part B or Part D depending on how it's used.

Substitutes come from a built-in list of about 50 common drug classes, or from Claude if you add an API key. This isn't medical advice: a close substitute can still differ in dose, timing, side effects, or interactions, so make any switch with your prescriber. The coverage check reads text, so it can miss a drug listed under another name, and it can't see your copay.
